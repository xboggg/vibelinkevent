— Chapter One —

In Which a Girl Discovers the Library

❦ · ❦ · ❦

Comfort Anyemedu was born in Anyako in 1950, the third of seven children of a fisherman and a midwife. She was the only one who finished school. She often told her own students that she did so because of a single library in Keta — one room with one shelf — where she read every book before she was twelve.

She read Shakespeare, Achebe, the King James Bible, a battered copy of Things Fall Apart with the cover missing, and a pamphlet about Soviet agriculture she could quote at length forty years later. From that one room she went to Wesley Girls', to Legon, to Yale, to a chair at the University of Ghana, and to seven novels that won every literary prize the country had.

"A good library is a window," she wrote. "A good library is also a door."

She was the principal librarian at Legon for fifteen years before she was made Professor of Comparative Literature. Her students adored her, dreaded her, and quoted her. She never married — but she raised her niece, Esi, who buried her last week. And she leaves behind hundreds of students who would now, if asked, count her among the most important people of their lives.

— Chapter Four —

In the Margins, Her Readers Write

❦ · ❦ · ❦

What is a life if not the marginalia of those who loved it? Below, some of the notes left in her copy.

"She made me rewrite an essay six times. The seventh I won a prize for. I have written every essay since with her standing over my shoulder." — Dr. Yaw Awoonor-Williams, '95
"Auntie Comfort taught me to read at five. I am now writing my second novel. The first was dedicated to her." — Esi Anyemedu, niece
"She never married — but she had hundreds of children. I count myself among them, gratefully." — Prof. K. Anyidoho
"The Balme Library will feel a quieter place. She lectured the books." — Mr. Antwi, librarian
