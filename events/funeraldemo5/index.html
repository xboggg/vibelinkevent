An Obituary

Bobo Mensa, the saxophone that never stopped.

For five decades he played highlife from the bandstand of every club worth knowing in Accra, Lagos and London. He died this week at 78, his horn at his side.

By the Editors · Photographs from the Mensa Family Archive

Albert Kofi "Bobo" Mensa, the saxophonist whose blistering solos defined the second wave of Ghanaian highlife and who, at his peak, was said to be able to make grown men weep with a single phrase, died on Tuesday at his home in Tema. He was 78.

His daughter Akua, who confirmed the death, said the cause was complications from a long illness he had refused to discuss in interviews. He had played his final public set just six weeks earlier, at a benefit for the Musicians' Welfare Fund.

Born in Cape Coast in 1947, Mr. Mensa first picked up the alto saxophone at thirteen, taught by an uncle who had played in the colonial police band. By twenty he was leading his own quartet. By thirty he had cut nine records, two of which — "The Heart Speaks" (1974) and "After Midnight" (1981) — are still considered foundational texts of Ghanaian jazz.

"Bobo did not play notes. He played sentences. You felt as if he was telling you something only you could hear."

He toured Europe four times in the 1980s, sharing stages with Manu Dibango, Hugh Masekela, and Dollar Brand. A 1986 review in The Guardian called his Brixton set "the most generous hour of music I have witnessed all year." But he always came home. "Ghana is where my horn was born," he told a interviewer in 2009. "I cannot leave my mother."

He is survived by his wife of 51 years, Margaret; four children — Kofi, Akua, Yaw, and Esi; nine grandchildren; and a tenor saxophone he named, simply, The Lady.

Letters to the Family

"He was ours, too."

He played at my wedding for the price of a meal.

"In 1992 I had no money but a band was the one thing I refused to skip. Bobo took the gig for a plate of jollof and refused payment after he saw my mother dance to his second song. I have been telling that story for thirty-three years."

— Ato Ankrah, Kasoa
My first sax was his old one.

"He gave me his alto in 1998 when he upgraded to the new tenor. I still play it. There is a small dent under the bell I have never repaired because it was already there when he gave it to me."

— Kojo Antwi (no, not that one)
He stayed sober the night my brother died.

"He came to the wake and played for four hours straight. He was famous for his drinking but that night not a drop. He just played. We have never forgotten."

— The Boateng Family
My father. My first audience.

"Every song I have ever recorded, I have imagined him in the back of the room nodding. Now he is there in person."

— Kofi Mensa Jr., eldest son
